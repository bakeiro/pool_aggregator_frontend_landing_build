The idea: make your Bitcoin work twice

This is a classic lending + farming strategy with a very specific goal: accumulating more Bitcoin.

The reasoning is simple. Bitcoin sitting idle in your wallet earns nothing. If you deposit it as collateral, you can borrow against it at a ridiculously low cost and put that loan to work in a liquidity pool. The collateral is still yours and still exposed to Bitcoin's price, and on top of that you have a second position generating fees.

The key lies in what you borrow and where you put it:

  • We borrow Bitcoin against Bitcoin. Debt and collateral move in exactly the same way.
  • We put it into a pool whose two tokens are almost perfectly correlated.

That way, the strategy doesn't depend on guessing where the market is heading.

What is CMC20 and why pair it with BTC?

CMC20 is an index token made up of 70% Bitcoin and 30% CoinMarketCap's top 20, excluding stablecoins.

pie title CMC20 composition
    "Bitcoin" : 70
    "Rest of the CoinMarketCap top 20 (no stablecoins)" : 30

With 70% BTC inside, its price moves practically in lockstep with Bitcoin: a 98–99% correlation. And that's exactly what we're looking for in a concentrated liquidity pool.

When two tokens move the same way, their relative price barely changes. That lets us set a very tight range, concentrate all our liquidity where the trading actually happens, and capture a much larger share of the fees, with hardly any impermanent loss.

Step 1: deposit Bitcoin on Aave

We connect the wallet to Aave on Binance Smart Chain and check the available BTCB. In our case, the maximum available was $98.97.

  1. We click Supply and select the maximum.
  2. We approve, sign, verify and confirm the transaction.

The deposited collateral now shows up on the left side of the interface: ~$99 in BTCB.

Step 2: borrow Bitcoin

On the right side (borrowing), Aave shows how much we can borrow: about $69, that is, up to 70% of the collateral, at an APR of 0.66%.

That interest accrues over the year and there is no maturity date: the debt can be repaid whenever you want.

The risk warning (and why it doesn't scare us)

When you select the maximum, the platform shows a warning: borrowing 70% of your collateral is a high level of debt.

In a regular loan —say, depositing BTC and borrowing stablecoins— you should take that warning very seriously: if Bitcoin drops, your collateral loses value while the debt stays the same, and you move closer to liquidation.

That doesn't happen here. We're borrowing BTC against BTC: the correlation is 100%. If Bitcoin falls 30%, the collateral falls 30%… and so does the debt. The relative price of one against the other doesn't change.

With that clear, we click Borrow BTC, verify, sign and confirm. The screen now shows the deposit on the left and the loan on the right.

Step 3: open the BTCB/CMC20 pool on Poolmaster

We go to Poolmaster, on Binance Smart Chain, search for "BTC" and select the pair with CMC20.

Before entering, the usual: look at the real history, not the last 24 hours' APR. In this case, the pool has averaged almost 45% over the last 30 days and almost 50% over the past year.

Setting the range

With two tokens this correlated, we could go for an extremely tight range. We prefer a slightly conservative stance: ±1%.

It's tight enough to concentrate liquidity and generate plenty of fees, and wide enough that we're not falling out of range every five minutes.

Execution

  1. We enter the ~$68 in BTCB from the loan.
  2. We enable auto swap, which takes care of converting the CMC20 portion.
  3. We approve the first token (the platform shows a warning, which we can ignore: it's completely trustworthy) and sign.
  4. We repeat for the second token.
  5. We click Create position, sign and confirm.

The portfolio now shows the position on Binance Smart Chain (PancakeSwap through Poolmaster) worth $68.06. Practically nothing lost along the way.

What do we do with the fees?

There's no single right answer here. There are three paths:

    F --> O2["2️⃣ Repay debt<br/>Improves the health factor"]
    F --> O3["3️⃣ Reinvest in the pool<br/>Compound interest"]

Option 1: accumulate more Bitcoin. This is the main goal. Every fee collected turns into more BTC in the portfolio.

Option 2: repay the debt. The interest is tiny, so mathematically there's no rush, but some people will prefer to chip away at the loan and sleep a bit easier.

Option 3: reinvest in the pool itself. With current incentives this high, letting compound interest do its job makes a lot of sense. Fees get added to the position, the position generates more fees, and later on we decide what to do with all of it.

Risk control: the health factor

On Aave, the health factor measures how far you are from liquidation. Our position sits at 1.8, and the interface flags it in red.

Again: no need to panic. That indicator is designed for loans between assets that move differently. With BTC against BTC, a price drop doesn't make it worse.

We also have two levers to improve it over time:

  • Adding more collateral (supply).
  • Repaying part of the debt (repay).

Both fit naturally with whatever we decide to do with the fees.

What we'll be watching

No strategy is perfect on paper, and this one isn't either. These are the points we'll be tracking:

  • Fees generated, days elapsed and real return. We'll log them and compare them with the pool's historical APR.
  • Time out of range. The ±1% is a decision, not dogma. If the price falls out of range too often, we'll consider widening it a little. A very high theoretical APR is worthless if the position spends half the day out of range while nobody is in front of the screen to rebalance.
  • CMC20's 30% altcoin share. The correlation is 98–99%, not 100%. During sharp market moves, the rest of the top 20 can drift further from BTC than usual, and that's exactly when a tight range gets tested.
  • Accrued interest. It's minimal, but it exists and keeps adding to the debt until it's repaid.

We'll publish everything, including what doesn't go as expected. That's the whole point of an educational portfolio.
